Understanding
the problem.

A premium brand undermined by its own platform.

40 years of global brand equity — invisible on the website. Black and white UI. No brand system. Navigation with zero hierarchy. Reviews buried below fold. Checkout visually detached from the rest of the site.

Phase 1 constraint: No functionality changes. No new features, no checkout flow rewrites, no recommendation engines, no personalisation. Every interaction, backend flow, and data structure stayed untouched. The only lever was design — visual language, layout, hierarchy, component quality.

How much conversion improvement can great design deliver without touching a single line of functional code?

Five structural failures,
one shared root cause

Heuristic audit against Nielsen's 10 principles, competitive analysis across five premium appliance brands, and Baymard Institute research as the quantitative foundation.

  1. Zero brand identity on platform

    Entirely black and white. No Haier brand colours, no visual system, no premium signals.

    A consumer familiar with Haier from retail would land and find nothing confirming they were on the right platform.

    Brand recognition and orientation

  2. Navigation with no hierarchy

    All paths carried equal visual weight. Product discovery was failing early in the funnel — users couldn't orient themselves or determine which categories were relevant to their needs.

    Weighted navigation and hierarchy

  3. Purchase-confidence signals buried

    Reviews sat below fold. CTAs competed with surrounding elements.

    The information hierarchy on product pages worked against how users actually make high-consideration appliance decisions.

    The purchase decision sequence

  4. Checkout visually detached

    Payment pages looked completely disconnected from the rest of the site — a pattern Baymard directly links to checkout abandonment. The brand thread broke exactly where trust mattered most.

    Continuity through checkout

  5. Mobile experience unoptimised

    Below-spec tap targets, thumbnails too small for high-value appliance evaluation, navigation requiring horizontal scroll. Failed at the first mobile moment of product consideration.

    Product evaluation on mobile

A brand trust restoration problem

Every failure traced to the same origin: the visual design actively communicated the opposite of what the Haier brand represents. Solvable entirely within Phase 1 — because trust is a design variable, not a functional one.

Redesigning
the purchase journey.

From a flat product catalog to a structured journey that orients, guides, and builds confidence at every stage. A full design system applied to optimised information architecture across every surface.

Before — original hierarchy

  1. Hero — no brand, black & white No identity
  2. Flat navigation, equal weight No hierarchy
  3. Price + weak CTA
  4. Long technical specs above fold Wrong order
  5. Reviews below fold
  6. Checkout — visually detached Trust break

After — decision sequence

  1. Brand-led hero + flash sale sub-header ↑ Identity
  2. Mega menu with weighted hierarchy ↑ Discovery
  3. Dominant CTA — primary action
  4. Featured reviews elevated above specs ↑ Confidence
  5. Specs below the decision trigger ↑ Clarity
  6. Checkout — brand-consistent ↑ Trust

A written comparison of the documented hierarchy.

  • Improved visual hierarchy — clear CTA dominance with brand colour system
  • Reduced promotional clutter — promotions integrated into hierarchy, not competing with it
  • Enhanced product discovery — mega menu with weighted category architecture
  • Strengthened trust signals — security indicators and reviews elevated to decision position
  • Optimised content flow — decision sequence restructured around how users actually evaluate appliances

A recognisable brand.
A clearer way in.

Brand-led hero, weighted mega menu navigation, persistent flash sale sub-header. The flash sale was the primary engagement hook — invisible below the fold in the original design.

Brand colour system applied end-to-end

Haier black as primary CTA colour, key navigational actions, and promotional signifiers. Critical application: checkout pages, previously disconnected from the rest of the site.

An unbroken visual identity thread from first impression to order confirmation.

Mega menu with sticky header and weighted hierarchy

Existing navigation links retained exactly — Phase 1 constraint. What changed: their visual architecture.

Primary appliance categories given highest visual weight. Sub-categories grouped.

Promotional links visually distinguished. Sticky header maintained access during long product page scrolls.

Reported homepage-to-category outcome: −20% bounce rate. See the published results and context.

Give the product
room to be evaluated.

The grid follows the purchase, not the other way around.

Mobile-first, WCAG 2.1 AA compliant component system

44px minimum tap targets. Single-column dominant product grids for major appliances — 2-column thumbnails were too small to evaluate product quality.

2-column retained for accessories. All colour contrast ratios verified against WCAG 2.1 AA.

Two-column thumbnails were too small to evaluate quality for large appliances. Grid logic matched the category and evaluation needs.

Product discovery was +40% faster in usability testing. See the published results and context.

Product pages rebuilt around
the purchase decision sequence.

Layout restructured: hero imagery → price and key specs above fold → dominant CTA → featured reviews → detailed specs → upsells. Reviews elevated from below specs to above — based on Baymard's finding that 95% of users rely on reviews during product evaluation.

Phase 1 constraint: No functionality changes. No new features, no checkout flow rewrites, no recommendation engines, no personalisation.

Baymard research context: the 95% figure describes external research cited in the original case study, not a HaierMall outcome.

Carry trust through
to payment.

Checkout trust signal overhaul within existing flow logic

Couldn't change checkout steps or logic. Changed everything the user sees.

Baymard enclosed field treatment. Trust seals above the payment input.

Persistent order summary. Full brand system through every checkout screen — eliminating the visual trust break at the most critical moment.

The flow stayed the same.
The visual trust break did not.

Baymard research context: the original case study cites 17% of checkout abandonment due to visual mistrust. This informed the enclosed fields, trust signals and brand continuity; it is not a measured HaierMall checkout improvement.

Results

and trustworthiness.

Return to the four verified outcomes

Trust is a design variable

Baymard research context, not an additional project metric.

17% of checkout abandonment is caused by visual distrust — a design problem with a design solution. Visual credibility is purchase permission, and it's entirely within the designer's control regardless of functional constraints.

Information hierarchy is conversion architecture

Placing reviews above specs wasn't aesthetic preference — it was restructuring the page to match how users actually make appliance decisions. Layout is the product.

Sequence is as consequential as the content itself.

Documentation is a design deliverable

Cross-cultural handoff revealed that naming intuitive to one team created ambiguity in another. A dedicated annotation layer should have been part of the design system from day one — not added mid-project.

Constraints sharpen the hypothesis

The prohibition on new functionality forced a precise question: how much conversion improvement can design deliver alone? The answer - 31% in 3 months — validates that great layout and hierarchy are not decoration.

They are the product experience.
