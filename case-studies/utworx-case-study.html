The Brief: Clarity Without Removing Capability

UTWorX is an enterprise SaaS platform for managing assets, maintenance and operational performance. As features and workflows grew, everyday tasks became harder to navigate. The brief was to simplify the interface without removing a feature or changing existing functionality.

From Stored Data
to Operational Decisions

The structured UX audit identified a system organized around the data it stored rather than the actions people needed to take. The task was to reduce cognitive effort, surface priorities and shorten navigation while preserving operational depth.

Information overload

Raw tables gave records similar visual weight. Without clear priorities, users had to read through entries to determine what needed attention.

Design response: bring summaries and priority signals ahead of detailed records.

See the dashboard response

Difficult navigation

The operational picture was spread across four to five screens. Work-order abandonment and supervisors falling back to phone or chat were described in the audit.

Design response: keep task actions within their working context and structure the work-order sequence.

See the workflow response

No dashboard visibility

Distributed data lacked synthesis. Managers were making decisions with an incomplete view of operational status.

Design response: combine task counts, charts and detailed work records in a single overview.

See the information hierarchy

Four principles for the redesign

  • Simplify data through visual summaries without losing depth.
  • Improve visibility of operational indicators without opening multiple sub-screens.
  • Streamline workflows through task-focused forms and contextual actions.
  • Modernize familiar patterns to support everyday enterprise work without unnecessary relearning.

Reading the Operation
at a Glance

The dashboard separates overview from investigation: work-summary counts establish status, charts make patterns visible, and the table retains the underlying work detail. The goal is to help managers identify where to look before reading individual records.

Different questions.
Different views.

Asset Overview and Spare Part Consumption sit above the work table. The consumption chart uses single-series bars with a Monthly/Weekly control; it is not a stacked-bar chart.

Navigation architecture: a persistent sidebar keeps modules available as the product grows. Top navigation would preserve more vertical space, but the sidebar follows familiar enterprise conventions such as Salesforce and ServiceNow.

Keeping Work Orders
in Context

Fault details, asset assignment, scheduling, personnel and documentation demand different kinds of attention. Instead of a single long form, the documented workflow divides that work into five named stages.

  1. Preliminary Report
  2. Notification Creation
  3. Work Scheduling
  4. Service Report
  5. Report Generation

Workflow summary from the project account; not a depiction of an on-screen progress indicator.

Capture the fault.
Then define the work.

The preliminary report collects observations, findings, attachments and follow-up. Notification creation adds the asset, category and priority so the next action has a defined context.

Coordinate the resources.
Close the loop.

Scheduling brings technician assignment, spare parts, date and estimated hours into one task. The service report records findings, components, parts used, completion state and supporting attachments.

Why contextual modals? Dedicated pages would move users away from the primary workflow for each secondary action. These forms keep the underlying work list in view. Reducing repeated navigation was the design intention; no measured time saving is claimed.

Planning Maintenance
Across Assets

Preventive maintenance requires an overview of assets and dates, then enough task detail to coordinate people, parts and completion. The table supports scanning; the task modal supports acting on one item.

Make completion
explicit.

The task form brings personnel and supporting records together. A success confirmation provides a clear end state after completion.

Bulk management and recurrence were part of the documented design intention. The available exports show an Upload Excel entry point and a task modal; they do not establish a complete bulk editor or inline assignment behavior.

Defining the Report
Before Reading the Records

Reporting starts by narrowing the question. Date, asset and work-status controls sit above the records, alongside Export. This replaces the need to begin with an undifferentiated long table and find the relevant information afterward.

The documented intent includes date ranges and open, completed or overdue work. The export shows the filter entry points, not their expanded option states.

The trade-offs
behind the interface.

Sidebar over top navigation

Top navigation offers more vertical space. A sidebar accommodates growing module counts and familiar enterprise navigation patterns. Familiarity was intended to reduce relearning.

Visual summaries alongside detailed tables

Improved sorting, filtering and colored rows would be a lower-risk change. Charts were chosen to make trends easier to interpret, while records remained available for detail. Faster insight was an objective, not a timed result.

Five focused stages over one long form

A single page could show every field at once. Separating fault, asset, scheduling, personnel and documentation tasks was intended to support focused attention and complete records, rather than prove a reduction in errors.

Contextual modals over separate pages

Separate screens would interrupt the main workflow for secondary tasks. Keeping actions near the work list was intended to reduce repeated transitions during sessions containing multiple work orders.

What enterprise design
reinforced.

Structure data rather than remove its depth

The same information can be organized around the decision it supports. Complexity is often a presentation problem rather than a reason to discard necessary records.

Familiarity can be a design advantage

For people working in the same tool for hours, precise use of established patterns matters more than novelty. The objective was to bring the work forward, with less effort spent interpreting the interface.

Hierarchy matters under time pressure

Priority signals, appropriate charts and contextual information were chosen to support decisions at the point of need. Their effect on speed and effort remains to be tested.

What I would
do differently.

The two-week timeline left an important gap: formal user testing. In retrospect, a half-day session with three to five current users on work-order creation and preventive maintenance would have helped replace assumptions with evidence. That session is a reflection, not completed research.

The redesign addressed presentation and workflow clarity. Predictive maintenance alerts, proactive scheduling suggestions and anomaly detection were outside scope. The original project account identifies existing platform data as a starting point for exploring that intelligence layer.
