Making Subscription
Decisions Easier

Subscribe Me launched as Dubai's first digital-native car subscription platform — one all-inclusive monthly payment covering vehicle, insurance, maintenance, and delivery. The product was compelling. The design problem was foundational: users were being asked to commit a recurring monthly payment through an interface they had never seen before, from a brand with no established digital reputation.

Every design decision had to resolve one of three core anxieties before users could move forward.

01

Pricing Transparency

Users couldn't parse which package covered what. "All-inclusive" meant different things across tiers, causing drop-off before users reached the booking flow.

Design response. Plan cards with full inclusion lists surfaced above fold. Every benefit visible before a user commits to evaluate.

See the design decision
02

Booking Friction

High-value subscription checkout creates commitment anxiety. Multiple decision points without visible progress led to mid-flow abandonment.

Design response. 3-stage named checkout with persistent progress indicator. Stage language chosen specifically to reduce anxiety at each commitment point.

See the design decision
03

Ongoing Management

After subscribing, users needed to manage services, request support, and handle vehicle issues — all through a platform with no existing operational interface.

Design response. Centralised dashboard combining bookings, services, documents, and support into a single management hub.

See the design decision

From Browsing
to Booking

The booking flow was structured to reduce anxiety at each stage — not just complete a checkout. Every screen answers one silent user question before they can move forward.

  1. Browse Cars
  2. Vehicle Details
  3. Packages
  4. Review Order
  5. Payment
  6. Confirmed

Orient the budget.
Then choose the car.

Category-first navigation — filter by budget, car class, and mileage — reduces cognitive load before users evaluate individual vehicles. Users orient their budget before committing attention to a specific model.

Design intent: reduce comparison overload.

Make the value
visible before commitment.

Pricing, specifications, and package benefits consolidated into one decision-making view. Key inclusions surfaced above fold so users understand total value before scrolling — addressing the "what am I actually paying for" anxiety directly.

A clear route
through commitment.

Stages 3–6: Package Selection → Review → Payment → Confirmation
Named progress steps (Create Account → Invoice Details → Booking Confirmed) make the endpoint visible. Cancellation terms surfaced at each commitment point — reducing the leading cause of subscription checkout abandonment.

The six stages describe the overall subscription journey, from discovering a car to confirming a booking.

Designing Beyond
the Booking

The experience extended beyond booking. Users needed a centralised hub to manage subscriptions, request services, and access support — without relying on manual customer service channels. A subscription a user actively manages is one they maintain.

Support belongs
in the everyday journey.

Services & Requests was elevated to primary navigation after the UX team's research confirmed it was the highest-frequency post-subscription action. One tap instead of two for the most common subscriber journey — a structural change with measurable impact on daily usability and support dependency.

Extending the
Design System

Created a reusable design system to ensure visual consistency across booking, dashboard, and support workflows — while accelerating future product development. Every component ships with all interaction states documented for zero-ambiguity dev handoff.

This contribution extended an inherited system during delivery. The original case study reports 50+ components with documented interaction states.

What the feedback
can tell us.

Selected positive reviews describe a straightforward booking experience. Critical feedback identifies car swaps, deposit delays and difficulty reaching support. These excerpts provide qualitative context; they do not establish the experience of every customer or isolate the impact of individual interface decisions.

The original case study identifies in-app dispute resolution and transparent billing history as a Version 2 design direction. These are future opportunities, not delivered features shown here. Operational problems also affect the overall customer experience.

What this project reinforced
about designing under pressure.

Design system literacy is as important as design skill

Joining at Sprint 4 demanded reading another designer's decisions and extending them faithfully. I now build a system-reading session into every project I join — regardless of whether I'm starting from scratch or inheriting existing work.

Evidence turns a preference into a recommendation

Defending the checkout progress bar in a stakeholder meeting taught me the difference between a design conversation and a design negotiation. Stakeholders can override opinions. Research is harder to dismiss.

Design doesn't end at handoff

The post-launch UI audit found cumulative visual drift — small decisions under deadline pressure, consequential in aggregate. A design system is only as valuable as its implementation.

The interface is the brand — when there's no other brand to lean on

For a new platform with no prior reputation, visual design quality was the primary trust proxy. Designing for trust through pricing clarity, commitment transparency, and visual consistency isn't a UX concern. It's a business one.

This project challenged me to balance complex subscription workflows with simplicity. Designing both the customer journey and the underlying design system reinforced the importance of scalable patterns, clear information architecture, and outcome-focused product design — within real professional constraints.
